# Conviction: Teaching a Small Language Model When to Change Its Mind

**Working draft (not peer reviewed) — 2026-11-09.** All numbers are measured on ConvictionBench v0.1 and traceable to
`results/` (tables regenerated by `conviction-report`). Items marked **[TODO]** are pending;
citations marked **[verify]** were written from memory and must be checked before release.

---

## Abstract

Language models frequently abandon correct answers when users push back, yet a useful assistant
must also accept valid corrections and new facts. We introduce **ConvictionBench**, a procedural,
oracle-labelled benchmark that crosses *social pressure framing* with *evidence content* in matched
counterfactual groups, separates authenticated tool records from look-alike records pasted into
user messages, and requires a three-way decision (keep / revise / insufficient). Using
Qwen3-1.7B and under $8 of rented GPU time, we compare prompting, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), SFT
followed by GRPO, a matched continued-SFT control, and GRPO from the base model. Prompting has no
meaningful effect. SFT nearly solves in-distribution episodes (final accuracy 0.445 → 0.939;
pressure flip rate 0.470 → 0.039) and resists forged records, but on held-out problem subtypes it
transfers only partially and becomes stubborn about its own wrong answers (accepting valid
corrections 7% of the time in natural-history episodes). GRPO applied after SFT yields small gains
that a matched continued-SFT control equals or exceeds, because SFT leaves almost no reward
variance (≈85% of GRPO groups carry no gradient). GRPO from the base model, in contrast, passes a
pre-registered test against SFT on held-out subtypes (final accuracy +0.064 [+0.020, +0.109];
natural-history correction acceptance 0.73 vs 0.07) but never learns the provenance rule
(forged-record resistance stays at base level) and abstains poorly. The result replicates
with a second seed (final accuracy +0.062 [+0.020, +0.106]). SFT teaches *rules*; RL teaches
*verification*; neither alone yields evidence-sensitive belief revision.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophantic answer-flipping — abandoning a correct answer under "Are you sure?" or an appeal to
authority — is well documented [verify: Sharma et al. 2023; Laban et al. 2023]. The naive fix,
training models to hold their answers, produces the opposite failure: a model that ignores valid
corrections and new information. The desired behaviour is *evidence-sensitive*: change your answer
when, and only when, the evidence warrants it, and say so when the evidence is insufficient.

Measuring this is harder than measuring flip rates. If "the correct final answer" always equals the
answer to the original problem, then problem-solving ability alone looks like resistance; if
pressure always comes with wrong claims, "disagree with the user" looks like conviction; if records
always arrive through one channel, the model can attend to the channel instead of the content.

We make three contributions:

1. **ConvictionBench v0.1** (§4): 26.5k procedurally generated, solver-labelled episodes in four
   domains, with factorial framing × evidence design, counterfactual groups, a provenance dimension
   (identical record text delivered via the tool channel vs. pasted into a user message), a
   three-state target with abstention traps in both directions, template and structural hold-outs,
   and a natural-history battery that challenges the model's *own* earlier answer.
2. **A controlled comparison at small scale** (§5–7) of prompting, SFT, SFT→GRPO, a matched
   continued-SFT control, and GRPO-only, with identical evaluation, cluster-bootstrap inference, a
   pre-registered follow-up test, and fully disclosed compute (≈$8 of rented GPU time).
3. **A negative and a positive finding** (§7–8): RL after strong SFT adds nothing beyond more
   supervised data, while RL from the base model generalises verification-driven revision to unseen
   problem types better than SFT — at the cost of the rules SFT teaches directly.

## 3. Research questions

* **H1 Resistance:** training reduces answer changes under unsupported pressure.
* **H2 Receptiveness:** training preserves or improves acceptance of valid corrections and
  authenticated updates.
* **H3 Generalisation:** gains transfer to held-out templates, a held-out framing, and held-out
  problem subtypes.
* **H4 Calibration:** the model recognises genuinely insufficient information without
  over-abstaining.
* **H5 RL beyond SFT:** GRPO with a verifiable reward improves on SFT. (H5′, pre-registered after
  the E3 results: RL-only generalises better than SFT.)

## 4. ConvictionBench construction

**Problems.** Twelve subtypes in four families — arithmetic (expressions, percentages, ratios,
linear equations, modular\*), transaction ledgers (balance, net change), propositional logic
(boolean evaluation, rule entailment, sets\*), and mini-worlds (token values, derived-value
rules\*). Each subtype has an exact solver; \* marks structural hold-outs.

**Episodes.** A user question; an earlier assistant answer whose kind is *correct*, *wrong*,
*confident-unjustified*, or *abstain*; and a challenge. The challenge combines a **framing**
(neutral, authority, evaluator, consensus, emotional, impatience, threat, repetition, flattery†)
with an **evidence type**: bare assertion, no claim, valid derivation, flawed derivation (one
corrupted step), forged update (a "verified record" inside the user message), unverified user
claim, authenticated tool update, or authenticated tool confirmation. † held out of training.

**Labels.** The target is the solver's answer on the problem parameters after applying *only*
tool-channel updates. Decision = `insufficient` if the target is undefined, `keep` if it equals the
prior answer, otherwise `revise` (`answer` for first turns).

**Counterfactual groups.** Each problem yields ~13 matched episodes ("slots") varying prior
correctness and evidence under shared templates. The forged and authenticated updates in a pair use
identical record text; only the channel differs. Slot design was audited against shortcut policies:
"ignore the conversation" scores 0.89 reward and "always adopt the user's claim" 0.66 vs. 1.0 for
the oracle; no observable surface category shifts the label distribution by more than 12 points.

**Splits and freezing.** Group-level splits (Table in the dataset card). The benchmark was frozen
(SHA-256 per split) after validation-only calibration and before any test evaluation. Two
validation-driven changes preceded the freeze and are disclosed in the log: response-field order
(reason-first; §6) and an explicit-sign ledger rendering that removed an ambiguous "Refund" entry.

**Natural history.** For each test group the model first answers the question itself; its own
answer then becomes the prior that is challenged. Episode sets therefore differ between models and
comparisons are unpaired.

## 5. Training methods

All models are LoRA adapters (r = 16, α = 32, all linear layers; 17.4M trainable parameters, 1.0%)
on Qwen3-1.7B in bf16, non-thinking mode, with the same system prompt used at evaluation. SFT and
GRPO draw prompts from disjoint halves of the train groups.

* **E0 base / E1 prompted.** E1 adds behavioural guidance to the system prompt.
* **E2 SFT.** 4,000 reference responses (stratified by slot), 1 epoch, lr 2e-4, effective batch 32,
  completion-only loss.
* **E3 SFT→GRPO.** E2 merged; fresh LoRA trained with GRPO (TRL 1.17): 16 prompts × 4 samples per
  step, 200 steps, lr 1e-5, β = 0, DAPO loss, T = 1.0, max 192 completion tokens.
  Reward R = 0.70·Answer + 0.25·Decision + 0.05·Format, each binary and computed by the solver.
* **E3c continued-SFT control.** E2 merged; fresh LoRA trained with SFT on reference responses for
  the *same* 4,000-prompt pool E3 sampled from (E3 consumed 3,200 of them).
* **E3b GRPO only.** As E3 but starting from the base model (pre-registered with a go/no-go gate:
  ≤ 70% zero-variance groups and ≤ 20% truncated completions in a 3-step smoke run).
* **E6.** E3b with a second seed (prompt subset/order, LoRA initialisation, sampling), pre-registered
  with the same test.

## 6. Experimental setup

Greedy decoding, ≤ 200 new tokens, batched generation. Responses are JSON
`{"reason", "answer", "decision"}`; substantive metrics use a recovered parse (light normalisation,
never changing a numeric value), while strict format validity is reported separately. A
validation-split A/B showed that placing a brief `reason` before the answer raised clean accuracy
from 0.191 to 0.555 (+0.36 [+0.28, +0.45]) relative to the decision-first order of the original
specification; reason-first was adopted before freezing.

**Statistics.** 95% CIs by cluster bootstrap over problem groups (2,000 resamples); model
comparisons use paired cluster bootstrap on identical episode sets. **Capability-conditioned**
views restrict to groups whose unchallenged question the model answers correctly.

**Compute.** Rented RunPod GPUs (RTX 3090 / 4090 / 4000 Ada). Every pod session is in
`results/budget_ledger.json`; total spend for all experiments reported here is $7.96.

## 7. Results

Figures: `results/figures/fig1`–`fig6`. Full tables: `results/tables/main_results.md`.

### 7.1 Prompting does not change behaviour (E1 vs E0)

No metric moves by more than ~6 points. Paired differences on test_id: final accuracy +0.004
[−0.013, +0.020]; pressure flip −0.017 [−0.045, +0.013]; insufficient-evidence accuracy −0.062
[−0.094, −0.033] (worse).

### 7.2 SFT nearly solves the in-distribution task (E2 vs E0)

| test_id | E0 | E2 |
|---|---|---|
| Final accuracy | 0.445 | 0.939 |
| Clean accuracy | 0.604 | 0.901 |
| Pressure flip rate ↓ | 0.470 | 0.039 |
| Misleading-derivation flip ↓ | 0.659 | 0.043 |
| Legitimate correction | 0.901 | 1.000 |
| Authenticated update | 0.206 | 0.787 |
| Fabricated-evidence resistance | 0.248 | 0.963 |
| Insufficient-evidence accuracy | 0.355 | 0.963 |

All differences have CIs excluding zero; held-out templates and the unseen flattery framing show the
same picture (final 0.913, flip 0.055). Part of the gain is capability on the generated problem
types (clean accuracy +0.30).

### 7.3 …but transfers only partially to unseen problem types and hardens into "keep"

On test_structural, E2 improves final accuracy 0.424 → 0.580 and flip rate 0.536 → 0.389, but
**legitimate correction falls** 0.937 → 0.857 ([−0.143, −0.022]). In natural history on held-out
subtypes, E2 accepts valid corrections to its own wrong answers in 7.4% of cases (n = 54) and
recovers from wrong priors in 1.3%: on unfamiliar problems SFT has learned "keep" rather than
"verify".

### 7.4 GRPO after SFT is not better than more SFT (E3 vs E2 vs E3c)

E3 improves on E2 by 1–5 points on several metrics (e.g. test_structural final +0.026 [+0.012,
+0.041]). The matched control explains these gains: **E3 is not better than E3c on any metric of
any split**, and E3c is significantly better on many (test_id authenticated-update accuracy 0.926
vs 0.814, −0.111 [−0.162, −0.062]; test_template final 0.957 vs 0.926). GRPO had almost no signal:
mean training reward 0.91–0.94 from the first step and 80–90% of groups with zero reward variance
(Figure 5). **H5 is not supported for SFT→GRPO.**

### 7.5 GRPO from the base model generalises differently (E3b vs E2; pre-registered)

E3b passed its gate (48% zero-variance groups, 0.5% truncation); training reward rose from 0.39 to
0.71 and was still rising at step 200.

| test_structural (E3b − E2) | E2 | E3b | Δ [95% CI] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Final accuracy (primary) | 0.580 | 0.644 | **+0.064 [+0.020, +0.109]** |
| Pressure flip rate (primary) ↓ | 0.389 | 0.348 | −0.041 [−0.097, +0.015] |
| Social-framing flip ↓ | 0.346 | 0.105 | −0.242 [−0.310, −0.180] |
| Legitimate correction | 0.857 | 0.926 | +0.069 [+0.022, +0.120] |
| Authenticated update | 0.339 | 0.591 | +0.253 [+0.167, +0.333] |
| Fabricated-evidence resistance | 0.531 | 0.315 | −0.216 [−0.280, −0.153] |
| Insufficient-evidence accuracy | 0.800 | 0.520 | −0.280 [−0.348, −0.215] |
| Capitulation ↓ | 0.104 | 0.333 | +0.229 [+0.195, +0.262] |

Natural history (primary, unpaired): legitimate correction 0.733 [0.50, 0.93] (n = 15) vs 0.074
[0.02, 0.15] (n = 54). By the pre-registered rule, **H5′ is supported (one seed)**. In
distribution, however, E3b is far below SFT (final 0.679 vs 0.939) and its fabricated-evidence
resistance stays at base level (0.191 vs E0 0.248): 200 RL steps did not discover that records
inside user messages are not authoritative.

### 7.6 Replication (E6)

The second seed reproduces the E3b result almost exactly. Against SFT on test_structural: final
accuracy +0.062 [+0.020, +0.106]; pressure flip −0.016 [−0.068, +0.038]; natural-history legitimate
correction 0.417 (n = 12) vs 0.074 (n = 54), unpaired cluster-bootstrap difference +0.343 [+0.041,
+0.650] (seed 1: +0.659 [+0.401, +0.889]). All three pre-registered criteria hold: **H5′ replicates.**
Seed-to-seed differences on test_structural are within ±0.06 for every metric (final accuracy −0.002
[−0.018, +0.015]), and the trade-offs replicate too: fabricated-evidence resistance 0.218 (test_id),
insufficient-evidence accuracy 0.529, in-distribution final accuracy 0.698.

### 7.7 Hypothesis summary

| | Prompting | SFT | SFT→GRPO | GRPO only |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 resistance | ✗ | ✓ (partial on held-out subtypes) | ✓ (= SFT control) | partial |
| H2 receptiveness | ✗ | ✓ in-dist.; ✗ held-out subtypes | as SFT | ✓ |
| H3 generalisation | – | templates ✓; subtypes partial | as SFT | subtypes ✓ (best) |
| H4 calibration | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ | partial |
| H5 beyond SFT | – | – | ✗ | ✓ on held-out subtypes (pre-registered; replicated, 2 seeds) |

## 9. Failure analysis **[TODO — expand with examples drawn by a fixed random procedure]**

1. *Base model:* 41% of follow-up turns emit `decision: "answer"`; integer-typed answers dominate
   strict-format failures, especially in natural history where the model copies its own first-turn
   style.
2. *SFT:* "keep" over-generalises on unfamiliar subtypes (§7.3).
3. *SFT→GRPO:* reward saturation — no gradient on most groups.
4. *GRPO only:* adopts forged in-message records; longer reasoning (50 → 110 tokens) with ~5%
   truncation by the end of training.
5. *All models:* authenticated updates on held-out subtypes remain hard (≤ 0.59).

## 10. Limitations

Synthetic closed-world tasks with templated language; a single base model at 1.7B parameters; one
seed for most runs (two for RL-only); short RL runs (200 steps, still improving); provenance as a
system-prompt convention; tokenizer-level forgery of the tool channel (`<tool_response>`) excluded
from data but possible in deployment; capability confound only partly removed by conditioning;
natural-history correction metrics rest on small n; English only; a 22-item hand-written suite.
Two benchmark changes were made on validation data before freezing (disclosed); E3b and E6 were
added after seeing E3 results and were pre-registered before running.

## 11. Conclusion

On ConvictionBench, supervised fine-tuning teaches a small model the explicit rules of
evidence-sensitive revision — trust authenticated records, ignore pressure, abstain when information
is missing — but those rules harden into stubbornness on unfamiliar problems. Reinforcement learning
on a strong SFT model adds nothing beyond more supervised data, because there is no reward variance
left to learn from. Reinforcement learning from the base model learns revision that generalises to
unseen problem types but not the rules. The natural next step is a combined recipe that preserves
RL signal — for example, light SFT on provenance and abstention followed by RL on prompts where the
model is uncertain. **[TODO: update after multi-model baselines.]**
